The Data on Data Privacy

Data has been described as the "currency of the 21st century." As more of our personal information becomes digitised, the need for our data to be respected and protected has become increasingly critical. Throughout this visualisation, examine the legal protections in place to protect data privacy in Australia and in Europe, consider enforcement measures taken for privacy breaches and explore how the Australian public's view on data privacy has evolved over time.

Australia: Legislative History and Protections

Federal Legislation

The most critical privacy legislation in Australia is passed at the Federal level, led by the Privacy Act 1988. The Privacy Act outlines which information is protected, penalties for breaches and importantly sets out the Australian Privacy Principles which govern how applicable business must handle personal information. Recent legislative amendments have increased penalties and introduced the statutory tort of serious breach of privacy, providing persons a new avenue to use to courts to pursue their rights. The National Health Act, Telecommunications Act and Criminal Code Act also contain some provisions related to data privacy within their respective fields.

State Legislation

The Australian states have taken varied approaches to passing their own privacy legislation. New South Wales were the first state to pass data privacy focused law, with the Privacy Committee Act 1975 predated the federal Privacy Act. Given the existence of the federal Privacy Act, state legislation seeks only to fill gaps that are specifically related to that particular state. For instance, the Victorian Privacy and Data Protection Act 2014 is limited to only cover the Victorian public sector. Hence, the late adoption of legislation in Western Australia and the absence in South Australia does not mean the people from these states were left without applicable data privacy legislation. Note: Not all legislation with reference to privacy is included, only dealing with major privacy enhancing law.

Australia: Views on Privacy

The plot above illustrates the substantially increasing concern that Australians have towards their data privacy compared only to five years ago. 86% of survey respondents indicated at least some elevated concern for their privacy, with only 2% more relaxed than half a decade prior. This rising level of concern is likely driven by rapid development of artifical intelligence, which brings with it major concerns about how personal and corporate information is being handled. Meanwhile, data privacy issues are getting more mainstream media attention, in line with the increased legislative focus.

The responses to these five questions paint a grim picture of how Australians feel their data is being protected. Between 2023 and 2026, there has been a steep increase in Australians valuing the protection of their personal information, and individuals have maintained a desire to have control over the use of their information. However, despite having these values, Australians overwhelmingly feel that they lack the control they desire, and many see this barrier as being too large to overcome, with 39% of Australians in 2026 deeming it too much effort to protect their data, and 36% resigning themselves to their data not being as private as they would like. These responses highlight the need for the continuing development of privacy law through amending the Privacy Act and state legislation, suggesting a stronger, more European-style approach may be welcome in Australia.

This bump chart illustrates the shift in priority in privacy concerns in Australia over time. Recognition of privacy risks is measured by how many Australians recognise each issue as a concern, so the more prominence a topic has in the public discourse, the higher it tends to be ranked as a concern. Artificial intelligence has risen from being the least important of these ten selected privacy risks in 2020 to rapidly rise into fourth place in the 2026 Community Attitudes to Privacy Survey, and it appears likely that it will continue to rise, especially in the wake of highly publicised indicents like the OpenAI Medicare hack. A reduction in the rankings does not necessarily mean concern in that area is falling, it is more common that it has simply been overtaken by other areas. Social media and location tracking remain major concerns for Australians, yet have become gradually more normalised and overtaken by more novel and extreme concerns.

This plot demonstrates that the overall concern for privacy issues has increased across all areas. Whilst the amounts differ, with a 26% increase for AI and only 4% for identity theft, on average the top 10 privacy risks in Australia have gone from having 51% of Australians concerned up to 65%. This increase is dramatic over only a three year period, and indicates a clear shift in Australian's values and worries, after changes were far less drastic between 2020 and 2023.

Europe: Stronger Protections and Enforcement

The European Union's GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) is held to be the most comprehensive data privacy legislation worldwide. It incorporates the right to access, rectify and erase personal data, empowers a higher standard of consent for data collection, and imposes serious penalties of up to 4% of worldwide annual revenues as fines for breaches (substantially stronger than anything available in Australia).

Under the GDPR, personal data may only be transferred to other countries if its protection can be guaranteed. A country is considered adequate for data transfers if it has roughly equivalent data protection legislation to the GDPR, otherwise data transfer can only occur under strict exemptions. Countries such as South Korea, Brazil and New Zealand are in the 14 adequate countries, whereas Australia is not. Canada is deemed adequate only for commercial organisations, while the US has an agreement for commercial organisations participating in the EU-US Data Privacy Framework agreement.

This map details the location and sector of each GDPR fine worth over one million Euros. The map illustrates that even in the short period in which the GDPR has been active, several major European nations have issued a multitude of fines, including Italy, France, Spain, Germany, Sweden, Ireland and the UK. Most of these countries have their fines spread across industries, however, all Irish fines are for media companies, such as Meta, TikTok, Google and LinkedIn. Major global technology companies have their European businesses set up in Ireland as it is natively English speaking, has close proximity to London and its Data Protection Commission is often considered to be lax. In all, a majority of EEA (European Economic Area) members have issued at least one major fine since 2019.

This scatterplot illustrates the largest GDPR fines, as issued over time and by sector. The largest fines in GDPR history are overwhelmingly large techology companies, which is natural since these are the largest companies in the world and fines are issued as a percentage of worldwide turnover. There does appear to be a small increase in enforcement both in number of fines and in quantum over time. Meta holds the title for the largest GDPR fine, as the only company to be fined over 1 million Euros in a singular penalty, fined for transferring European user data to the US with less than the necessary protections. Uber in August received the second largest fine for algorithmically disabling user accounts without proper human process or oversight.

The map above illustrates the total quantum of GDPR fines issues in each EEA nation, plotted on a square root scale. Comparing this to the dot map above, it is clear that the total number of fines issued and the total amount fined do not necessarily correlate. For instance, the UK has issued more major GDPR fines than Ireland. However, the total volume of Ireland's fines is almost 40 times that of the UK. This is explained by the UK having a more diverse variety of corporations, whereas Ireland is the official corporate home of the largest technology companies. Likewise, Spain issues a comparable number of fines as France, yet has accrued less than one seventh of the fine revenue.

This donut plot demonstrates in more comparative detail the magnitude of Ireland's fines in comparison to the rest of Europe. Implicitly, this shows the immense proportion of worldwide business revenue that the very large technology companies have over the rest of the market. In all, 10 of the top 12 GDPR fines of all time originate in Ireland. The Netherlands and France achieve large shares also through fining these companies. Uber in Europe is based out of Amsterdam, and two fines for Uber comprise the remaining two fines in the top 12. Meanwhile, Meta and Google also have major operations in France, which have led to major penalties there, albeit not as significant as the actions taken in Ireland.

Conclusions

Summary

It is clear that Australians are growing increasingly concerned about threats to their data privacy, and that there is little faith that much is being done to ease these concerns. Despite having a variety of legislative protections at both state and federal level, data privacy protection in Australia is not deemed 'adequate' by GDPR standards in Europe, the gold standard for data privacy regulation. While Australia may not have the global influence to issue penalties as extreme as in Europe, there is clearly a desire from the public to push towards a more privacy-prioritised approach. Legislating to ensure Australia reaches the 'adequate' standard would be an appropriate step to easing these concerns.
